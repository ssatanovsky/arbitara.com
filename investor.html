The management decision is the last unmanaged organizational object.

The problem

The most consequential act in an organization is the least managed.

Organizations map their processes, model their architectures, and instrument every operation with dashboards. Then they make their most consequential choices — market entries, major capital, senior appointments, reorganizations — in a room, with no structure, no record, and no way to learn from what happens next. Ask any executive three questions and the answer is the same: where are the options you considered recorded? Where do the assumptions behind a three-year commitment live? Who owns the decision to revisit it when the market moves? Nowhere, nowhere, and no one.

This is not a failure of intelligence. It is a failure of infrastructure. Organizations lack a system of record for management decisions the way they once lacked one for customers, processes, and architecture — until Salesforce, Signavio, and LeanIX made those objects visible and governable. The decision is the last major organizational object without a home.

The solution

A system of record for the management decision.

Illustration — the Decision Object lifecycle: framing, options, criteria, assumptions, owner, outcome.

Arbitara turns each consequential decision into a Decision Object — a structured, persistent record that captures the full lifecycle: the framing, the options evaluated (including do-nothing), the criteria applied, the assumptions recorded with review dates, the accountable owner, and the outcome tracked over time. It is not a place to write down what was decided. It is infrastructure that makes the decision visible, queryable, and permanently connected to the strategy it serves.

Not a toolA system of record
Helps one person decide better onceMakes the decision an organizational asset that persists after the meeting, the quarter, and the person who made it
Output is a documentOutput is a governed object with an owner, a lifecycle, and a link to strategy
Forgotten when conditions changeAssumptions carry review dates; the record signals when a commitment needs revisiting

AI advises. The human decides. Always. Arbitara's intelligence layer structures the choice, surfaces analogous past decisions, monitors assumptions, and makes bias visible — but it never selects the criteria, recommends the option, or carries the accountability. The choice stays with the person who can be questioned and bears the consequences. That governance commitment is what makes the platform adoptable at board level, where an autonomous "decision engine" is not.

Why now

Three forces, already operating — not forecast.

ForceWhy it makes the category inevitable now
AI crossed the thresholdIn the last three years, models became able to structure, connect, and query unstructured decision records — the enabling technology that did not exist when the problem was first felt.
Governance pressure is operating, not predictedBoards are asking questions today they could not enforce two years ago: show me the options, the assumptions, the accountability. The demand side is already live.
Organizational complexity is compoundingDistributed workforces, matrix structures, and accelerated M&A mean more consequential decisions than ever are made by people with no structured access to the rationale behind the last one.

These forces don't create the market — they make it visible. The category is not about to emerge. It is about to be named.

Why we win

Capable neighbors, an empty center.

Illustration — the five competitive rings, and the empty center Arbitara occupies.

The landscape is not empty. It's full of capable, well-capitalized platforms solving adjacent problems — none of them the one Arbitara solves. The management decision as a structured, persistent, governable object belongs to no one.

RingPlayersWhy they don't own the decision
Adjacent infrastructureSignavio · LeanIX · CelonisValidated the pattern of making invisible organizational assets visible and governable — for processes and architecture, not decisions.
Strategy & planningCascade · Quantive · WorkboardTrack goals, OKRs, and execution metrics — the outputs of decisions, not the decisions that produced them.
BI & analyticsPower BI · Tableau · LookerAnswer what happened, with no mechanism to connect outcomes back to the decisions, options, and assumptions that created them.
AI productivityNotion AI · Microsoft Copilot · ChatGPT EnterpriseGenerate output on demand — no persistent record, no structured object, no governance layer.
Operational AI decisioningFICO · IBM · SAS · Aera · Pega · Oracle · Quantexa · ACTICO (Gartner MQ, Jan 2026)Automate computable decisions with defined rules — not the judgment calls management makes under genuine uncertainty.

The moat is time, not features. Each of these players could add a "decisions" feature — none can retroactively own the object. Arbitara's defensibility compounds: every Decision Object an organization records deepens an institutional memory that lives nowhere else, and three years of connected decisions is not a dataset a CFO authorizes migrating to a competitor. The category-creation playbook is deliberate — name the object with precision, demonstrate the cost of its absence, and build the system of record (not a tool) that becomes professionally indefensible to operate without, exactly as CRM did.

Business model

Priced by governance complexity, not seats.

The unit of value is the Decision Object, not the seat. A CHRO who opens the platform once a quarter to close a People-domain decision is not less valuable to capture than an analyst who logs in daily — seat pricing underprices exactly the accounts generating the most institutional value. The expansion thesis. Arbitara enters through a single function — Strategy, Finance, or the C-suite office — and spreads as the decision-governance problem becomes cross-functional, because a decision made in one function references assumptions tracked in another. When a second business unit joins, it isn't buying seats to an existing tool — it's joining an institutional memory that already exists. Three years of connected Decision Objects is not data a CFO authorizes migrating to a competitor. That asymmetry is what produces NRR above 120%.

Go-to-market

A specific buyer, not an aspirational segment.

The beachhead buyer is specific, not aspirational. The target is the Chief Strategy Officer or Head of Transformation at a $500M–$5B organization that has already invested in process or enterprise-architecture governance — Signavio, LeanIX, or equivalent — and feels the gap where decision governance should be. This buyer isn't discovering the category from scratch. They're recognizing it, which eliminates the most expensive phase of enterprise sales: educating a buyer that the problem matters.

Three conditions make this buyer tractable: adjacent budget (an existing governance/transformation line, not a new one to create), organizational authority (a top-down mandate, not bottom-up adoption), and the vocabulary (structured organizational objects and governance layers are already familiar from Signavio/LeanIX implementations).

The sales motion is direct, high-touch, and category-creating. No channel shortcut works at this stage — the sale needs someone who can articulate the problem at the governance level and close at a contract value that justifies a 12–18 month cycle. That length isn't dysfunction; it's the correct calibration for a platform requiring CEO or board-level mandate. In parallel, category-creation content is commercial infrastructure, not marketing: before a CSO can name decision governance to their board, analysts and practitioners need to have already named it.

This is not a category without precedent for the outcome. The two closest structural analogues — platforms that made an ungoverned organizational object visible and governable — were both acquired by the same strategic buyer, SAP, for approximately €1.2 billion each. Arbitara is building the same kind of system of record for a higher-consequence object.

Stated plainly as a thesis, not a promise: at the growth trajectory above, Arbitara reaches the $150–200M ARR band that, at the ~6× revenue multiple these comparables commanded, implies a €1.2–1.5B strategic exit. The natural acquirers are the same platform vendors consolidating the governance layer.
